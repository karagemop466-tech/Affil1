How to assess cleanup and counter-space trade-offs

The appliance you stop using is the one that is annoying to clean or annoying to store. Both are decidable before purchase, from the listing, if you know which lines to read.

Crisp & Counter editorial· 5 min read· Updated 2026-11-20· Affiliate links, disclosed above

Two things retire an air fryer early. The basket is a nuisance to wash, or the machine has nowhere to live. Neither is a mystery at the point of sale. Both are visible in the listing, and both are decided by your kitchen rather than by the product.

Cleanup: read the material claim, not the marketing claim

Every listing will say something about easy cleaning. The sentence worth finding is narrower: which parts are dishwasher-safe. A nonstick coating that is dishwasher-safe still degrades faster in a dishwasher than in a sink, which is a trade-off you are allowed to make knowingly.

Ask these, in this order:

  • Which specific parts does the listing call dishwasher-safe — the basket, the crisper plate, the racks, the accessories?
  • Is there a removable tray or plate, or is the basket one piece? A removable plate is one more thing to wash and one less thing to scrub.
  • Are there exposed heating elements or a fan guard that food can reach? Those are the surfaces that turn cleaning into a project.
  • Does the door or drawer come off entirely? An oven-style door that does not detach is harder to clean than one that does.
  • Where does the grease go? A drip tray is a gift. A bare cavity floor is not.

A note on what we will not say

We will not tell you a coating lasts a certain number of years, or that one machine is easier to clean than another. We have not washed any of them. What we can tell you is what each listing claims about dishwasher-safe parts, and you can weigh that yourself.

Counter space: the measurement people skip

Footprint is not length by width. It is length by width plus the space the machine needs to be used:

  1. Base footprint — where it sits. Measure with a tape, not by eye.
  2. Door or drawer swing — the arc it needs when open, plus where your body stands.
  3. Vent clearance — the gap the manufacturer asks for behind and above. This is usually in the manual, not the listing, which is why it is worth knowing before you unbox.
  4. Cord reach — the distance to the nearest socket, and whether that socket is on the same circuit as something else.
  5. Storage, if it does not live out — a machine you have to lift into a cupboard is a machine you stop using.

Where the trade-off actually lands

Drawer machines trade capacity for a footprint that is small and predictable. Front-door ovens buy capacity and multi-level cooking with a bigger footprint and a swing arc. That is the whole trade. Everything else is detail.
