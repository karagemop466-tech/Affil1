Sizing

A no-hype checklist for comparing air-fryer capacity

The quart figure on the box answers a question you did not ask. This is the version of the question you did ask.

Crisp & Counter editorial desk · Updated 2026-11-20 · 6 min read · no product tested · no prices or ratings

A quart is a volume. You cook with a surface.

Capacity is quoted as a volume because a volume is one number and numbers fit on boxes. But hot air needs to reach the food, and food that is piled up does not get air on the parts that are touching.

So the useful question is not 'how many quarts' but 'how much food can sit in a layer that still lets air around it'. Those two questions have different answers, and the second one is not printed anywhere.

What to do instead, in order

1. Pick the actual meal
Not 'food' in general. The specific thing you will cook on a Tuesday: six chicken thighs, a bag of frozen chips, two salmon fillets.
2. Count the pieces
Write the number down. This converts a vague intention into something you can compare against a drawing.
3. Look for the usable area
Some manuals show the basket or rack dimensions, or a diagram with food laid out. That drawing is worth more than the quart figure.
4. Check whether it needs a single layer
Most air-frying guidance assumes space around each piece. If your meal needs a single layer, the layer is your real capacity.
5. Multiply by batches
If your meal does not fit in one layer, the honest capacity is 'two batches', and you should decide now whether you mind waiting.
6. Take the measurements off the listing
External width, depth and height, so you know it fits the gap. If they are not published, that is a reason to look at a different listing, not a reason to guess.

The trap in the number

A larger quoted capacity usually means a physically larger machine, which needs more counter and, in the oven shape, more door clearance. There is no configuration where you get more cooking space for the same footprint.

The other trap is the opposite one: buying small to save counter space and then discovering that the meal you cook most often needs two batches. Two batches is not a disaster, but it is a daily tax, and it is worth noticing before you pay it rather than after.

What the sellers in this register actually state

All five submitted links resolved to listings on re-verification 2026-11-20 (the fifth previously returned only the continue-shopping interstitial). Three are air fryers; two are probe thermometers, which have no capacity to compare. The three titles state 6 quarts across two 3-quart baskets (P-01), 8 quarts in a single basket (P-03), and 26 quarts in a French-door oven (P-02).

Those are the sellers' figures, repeated as figures. They have not been measured. A quart quoted for a two-basket machine is also counting two separate containers, which is exactly the kind of detail a single capacity number hides — so if you are comparing P-01 against P-03, compare the 3-quart basket against the 8-quart basket, not the 6 against the 8.

When the number is genuinely enough

If you cook for one or two, mostly loose food, mostly from frozen, and you do not mind a second batch occasionally, then almost any capacity will work and this checklist is more caution than you need.

The checklist earns its keep when three things are true at once: you cook for more than two, the food needs to lie flat, and counter space is tight. That combination is where the quart figure misleads the most.
