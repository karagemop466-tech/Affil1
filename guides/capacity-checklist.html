Capacity · 5 min read

A no-hype checklist for comparing air-fryer capacity

Quarts are a unit of volume. Portions are a unit of dinner. Here is how to move between them.

The Good Measure DeskEditorial deskPublished 2026-11-20 · Updated 2026-11-20

A capacity number on a box is a volume, not a promise. “6 quart” could describe two 3-quart baskets or one 6-quart cavity, and those two appliances cook very different dinners. The marketing is the same; the result is not.

Step 1 — split the number before you compare it

Write the basket count next to the volume. A 6-quart single basket holds one round of roughly six chicken pieces. A 6-quart dual basket holds two independent 3-quart halves, so you get two smaller rounds at the same time, not one large one. Both are “6 quart”. Only one of them is right for a family of four.

Step 2 — convert quarts to a familiar object

A 3-quart basket fits about three or four bone-in chicken pieces, a side of fries, or a small whole fish. An 8-quart basket fits a roast-sized piece with room around it. A 26-quart oven cavity fits a tray of anything. Write the number down against your own usual dishes rather than trying to remember who many portions it is.

Step 3 — check the space you did not account for

Clearance above the unit, the pull-out depth needed for the drawer, and the shelf space required to store it. A capacity you cannot store is capacity you cannot use. Measure the alcove, not just the counter.

Step 4 — price the second round

A smaller basket that fits your table on one round is usually the better buy for one or two people than a larger one that needs two rounds to feed the same table. Capacity you have to use twice is not capacity you have.
