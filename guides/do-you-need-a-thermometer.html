Do you need a thermometer to get good air-fried results?

Air fryers cook hot and fast. That is exactly when a probe earns its price.

The Good Measure DeskEditorial deskPublished 2026-11-20 · Updated 2026-11-20

An air fryer's marketing promise is speed. A chicken thigh that is properly cooked — browned outside, “72 degrees” inside — takes longer than the box suggests, because the box suggests a time, not a temperature. Speed is how the appliance cooks. Doneness is what you eat.

The clock is the weak link

Air frying combines fast-moving hot air with a small cavity and a short distance between the element and the food. That combination produces excellent browning and, at the same time, a real gap between the outside and the inside of a thick piece. A thigh that looks done on the outside can still be undercooked in the middle, and no timer catches that.

What a probe changes

A probe turns “brown outside, cooked through” from an impression into a reading. Three probes let you watch a roast and two sides at once. The listed 1000-degree ceiling is well beyond anything a home air fryer reaches, so the probe is not the limiting factor — the heat settings on the appliance are.

When you do not need one

If you mostly cook thin, small items — fries, wedges, chicken bites, vegetables — the cooking time is short and consistent and a timer is genuinely enough. The probe earns its keep on thick, high-risk pieces: whole chicken, pork shoulder, thick steaks, casseroles.

What to look for

Probe thickness decides how much of the food you can read: an ultra-thin probe can go into a chicken breast without cutting it in half. Wireless means you can leave the probe in and carry the dish to the table. Check the app and alert behaviour before buying, because that is where the value sits or falls.

Before you rely on this
