Reheating leftovers so the outside is not the only hot part

Cold middle, rubbery edge, and a dish that has been microwaved twice. Here is what changes that, what the food-safety guidance actually asks of you, and where a countertop fryer fits.

Updated 2026-11-20 · Crisp & Counter

An editorial illustration for the reheating problem. It is not a photograph of any model sold on this site.

Why the microwave fails, in one sentence

The published guidance is blunt about the mechanism: microwaves have cold spots, so the advice is to cover the food, rotate it, and check the temperature in several places with a food thermometer, allowing a resting time before you check again. A dense dish and a thin one do not reheat at the same rate, and the guidance says so — cooking continues longer in dense food than in breads, small vegetables and fruit.

That is the whole argument for a dry hot environment when the thing you care about is texture, and for a thermometer when the thing you care about is safety. They are two separate problems that get conflated constantly.

What a countertop fryer changes, and what it does not

A small, forced-hot-air cavity is good at one thing: putting heat onto a surface fast enough that moisture leaves it before the inside turns to steam. Crisp skins, crusts, breaded things, roasted vegetables that went soft. It is not good at liquids, and it will not rescue a saucy dish — that is a pan, and the leftovers guidance for sauces and gravies is a rolling boil.

It also does not change the safety rule. Reheated leftovers should reach 165°F as measured with a food thermometer, covered, whether they came out of a fryer, an oven or a microwave. A crisp exterior is not evidence of anything.

Good candidates
Fried or roasted things with a surface: potatoes, wings, breaded cutlets, pizza, rice with a crust wanted on it, pastry.
Poor candidates
Anything wet, anything custardy, anything that has to stay soft: soups, stews, steamed vegetables, plain rice, most pasta.
The step people skip
Check it. In several places, with a probe, for thin food inserted sideways so the sensing area reaches the centre.

The size question nobody puts on the box

A reheating batch has a different shape from a cooking batch: it needs to lie flat in one layer, because a pile steams. If you are buying capacity for reheating, compare the usable floor of the basket or tray against the plate you actually eat from — not against a quart number. The single-basket machine in this edit is described by its listing as 8 quarts, and the dual-basket one as two independent 3-quart baskets, in the seller's wording; three quarts is a small dinner for two people who want one layer each.

If the reheating problem is your real problem, count portions rather than litres, and check whether the parts go in a dishwasher, since reheating four times a week means washing four times a week.
