Chicken in an air fryer: how do you know it is actually done?

Browning arrives early in a small, fast, windy oven. The advice that has stood for decades is a number, a placement and a warning about trusting colour.

Updated 2026-11-20 · Crisp & Counter

Illustration commissioned for this guide. Not a photograph of a product sold here, and not a record of a test.

Why the small machine makes this harder

A basket fryer puts a heating element and a fan close to a small amount of food. Skin colours, edges crisp and the room starts to smell finished. What the surface is doing is not a reliable report about the middle.

This is not an air-fryer problem, it is a chicken problem that an air fryer makes more tempting. The USDA's food-safety service puts it directly: colour and grill marks are not indicators that food is safe to eat, and the only way to know food has been cooked to a safe internal temperature is to use a food thermometer.

The number, in the USDA's own words

These are the safe minimum internal temperatures from the FSIS fact sheet, transcribed rather than paraphrased. There is nothing air-fryer-specific about them, which is the point: the appliance changes how fast you get there, not where there is.

Read from the FSIS 'Food Thermometers' fact sheet on 2026-11-20. The same page notes that most pathogens are destroyed between 140 and 165 degrees F, and that people may cook to higher temperatures for preference.

Where the probe goes, and why a drumstick is fussier than a breast

A thick piece
In the thickest part, avoiding bone, fat and gristle. For poultry parts specifically, FSIS says to insert the thermometer into the thickest area, avoiding the bone.
A thin piece
Through the side, not from the top. The whole sensing area has to reach the centre, which for an instant-read thermometer is usually 2 to 3 inches - a thin chicken breast is shallower than that, which is exactly why people get wrong answers.
A hot piece
Take it off the heat. FSIS suggests removing the food from the pan or basket, laying it on a clean spatula or plate and inserting the probe sideways, rather than burning your fingers over a basket.
More than one piece
Check each one. Size and how the pieces were arranged in the basket means they reach temperature at different times.

What the basket has to do with it

A basket is a small cavity with one airflow path. How your machine behaves when it is half full rather than shallow, and whether a rack may be used in a given mode, are questions the manual answers and a review section does not, reliably.

The one purchase that settles the question

There are two shapes of answer. An instant-read thermometer goes in at the end and tells you once: it is cheap, it is fast, and it forces you to open the basket. A leave-in probe sits in the food while it cooks and tells you continuously, which is the difference between guessing when to look and being told.

One caution the USDA attaches to leave-in probes applies to any of them: do not leave a food thermometer in the food while it cooks unless the manufacturer's instructions specifically say it can withstand heat and it was designed for that purpose. A probe sold as a leave-in probe is a different object from a fold-out instant-read, whatever it looks like.

Three questions, on the plate

What is the piece?
Thigh, breast, wing and ground poultry are all poultry, and all 165 degrees F. A whole bird is checked in three places.
Where did the probe go?
Thickest part, off the bone, sensing area fully in the food. Through the side if the piece is thin.
What did it read?
165 degrees F. Not a feel, not a colour, not a timer on an app. A number, from the middle.
