Before you buy

Questions to ask before buying a multi-function kitchen appliance

Multi-function usually means one thing done well and several things done acceptably. The trick is finding out which is which before it takes up a shelf.

Updated 2026-11-20 · 6 min read · no product tested · no prices or ratings

Start by naming the one job

Every multi-function appliance has a job it was designed around and a list of jobs it will also attempt. The list is what the marketing talks about. The job is what you will actually use it for.

Before comparing anything, write one sentence: 'I want this to make ___ easier.' If you cannot finish that sentence without listing three equally weighted things, you are looking at a category where one good single-purpose appliance and one good pan will serve you better.

Nine questions, in the order that saves time

What is the primary job, in the seller's own words?
The first clause of a listing title is usually the job. Everything after the first pipe or comma is the ambition.
How many of the functions need a separate accessory?
If three of the advertised functions need parts that live in a cupboard, you have bought a machine and a drawer.
What does the manual say about each function?
Presets are not the same as capability. A preset is a button that sets a time and a temperature; it does not tell you the result is good.
How large is it, and where does it go?
An appliance that does four things but lives in a cupboard does one thing: take up space.
How many pieces come out to wash?
Count them for the function you will use daily, not the one you will use at Christmas.
Does it need clearance above or in front?
Doors that open out and lids that lift both consume the space you stand in.
What is excluded from the warranty, and for how long?
Read the actual document, not the badge on the box. If the document is not published, that is information.
What happens when one part breaks?
Accessory availability is the difference between a five-year appliance and an eighteen-month one.
Would two simpler things do the job better?
The correct answer is sometimes yes, and a multi-function appliance is often the wrong purchase for someone who already owns a good oven.

The preset trap

A count of presets is the easiest number to put on a box and the least informative. Twenty-four presets can be twenty-four combinations of the same two settings on the same heating element.

When a listing leads with a preset count, read the rest of the title for the things that constrain the experience: the size of the cavity, the number of racks, whether the door opens flat, how many accessories are included. Those are the parts you cannot change later.

A decision rule that holds up

Buy for the job you do weekly. Rent, borrow or use a pan for the job you do twice a year.

If a multi-function appliance is the only way to get the weekly job done in the space you have, it is the right purchase. If it is a way to avoid choosing, it will end up in the cupboard by March.
