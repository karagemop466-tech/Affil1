Questions to ask before buying a multi-function kitchen appliance

Multi-function usually means one thing done well and several done acceptably. The trick is finding out which is which before it takes a shelf.

Updated 2026-11-20 · Crisp & Counter

Illustration of three appliance shapes, none of them a product sold here.

Start by naming the one job

Every multi-function appliance has a job it was designed around and a list of jobs it will also attempt. The list is what the marketing talks about; the job is what you will actually use it for.

Before comparing anything, finish this sentence: “I want this to make ___ easier.” If you cannot finish it without listing three equally weighted things, one good single-purpose appliance and one good pan will serve you better.

Nine questions, in the order that saves time

What is the primary job, in the seller's own words?
The first clause of a listing title is usually the job. Everything after the first pipe is the ambition.
How many functions need a separate accessory?
If three advertised functions need parts that live in a cupboard, you have bought a machine and a drawer.
What does the manual say about each function?
A preset is a button that sets a time and a temperature. It is not evidence that the result is good.
How large is it, and where does it go?
An appliance that does four things but lives in a cupboard does one thing: take up space.
How many pieces come out to wash?
Count them for the function you will use daily, not the one you will use at Christmas.
Does it need clearance above or in front?
Doors that open out and lids that lift both consume the space you stand in.
What is excluded from the warranty, and for how long?
Read the document, not the badge on the box. If it is not published, that is information too.
What happens when one part breaks?
Accessory availability is the difference between a five-year appliance and an eighteen-month one.
Would two simpler things do it better?
Sometimes yes. This is the question the listing will never ask you.

The preset trap

A count of presets is the easiest number to print on a box and the least informative. Twenty-four presets can be twenty-four combinations of the same two settings on the same heating element.

When a listing leads with a preset count, read the rest of the title for the things that constrain the experience: cavity size, rack count, whether the door opens flat, how many accessories are included. Those are the parts you cannot change later.

Where a thermometer fits, and where it does not

P-05 is not an air fryer. It is a wireless meat thermometer whose listing states three probes and a 1000°F heat rating, and which names air fryers among its intended uses. P-04 is the instant-read version of the same idea.

That is a useful illustration of the multi-function question rather than an exception to it. A probe does one job — it tells you the internal temperature of something — and it does that job in an oven, a pan, a smoker or an air fryer. A single-purpose tool that travels between appliances is often a better purchase than an appliance that claims to do the thermometer's job as one of nine features.

A decision rule that holds up

Buy for the job you do weekly. Borrow, or use a pan, for the job you do twice a year.

If a multi-function appliance is the only way to get the weekly job done in the space you have, it is the right purchase. If it is a way to avoid choosing, it will be in the cupboard by March.
