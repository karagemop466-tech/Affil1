Thanksgiving without the oven traffic jam: what to move, and what never leaves

A countertop fryer will not save the bird. It can take four sides, the rolls and the reheating off an oven that is already busy for hours. Here is the plan, in the order you actually make it.

Updated 2026-11-20 · Crisp & Counter

An editorial illustration for holiday timing. It is not a photograph of any model sold on this site.

Plan backward from the bird, not forward from the appliance

The fixed point on Thanksgiving is the turkey, and the government guidance around it is not negotiable by schedule: a whole turkey is safe when it reaches a minimum internal temperature of 165°F measured with a food thermometer, checked in the innermost part of the thigh, the innermost part of the wing and the thickest part of the breast. The cooking timetable published with that advice assumes a 325°F oven, and it is written to be used alongside a thermometer rather than instead of one.

So the useful question is not “can the air fryer cook dinner”. It is “which dishes do not need to be under 325°F dry heat for an hour, and can therefore leave the big oven entirely?” Write the menu in two columns before you touch a specification sheet.

Two columns: under 165°F or near it

Column one is everything that has to hold one temperature for a long time: the bird, a gratin that needs to set, a pie that cannot be rushed. That column stays in the main oven. There is no clever workaround, and a countertop cavity does not change the arithmetic.

Column two is anything already cooked that only needs to be crisped, browned or driven back up to temperature: roasted vegetables that went soft, rolls, potatoes with a crust on top, frozen appetisers, bacon, leftover mash with something browned on it. That column is where a second heat source earns its counter space.

One line belongs in column two even if it surprises you: reheated leftovers. The federal guidance for leftovers is to reheat them to 165°F, covered, and to bring sauces, soups and gravies to a rolling boil. A small hot cavity reaching that temperature quickly is doing exactly the job the guidance asks for.

What the appliance cannot take over
Anything whose doneness you cannot verify with a probe, and anything bigger than the cooking surface you measured before you bought it.
What it can genuinely take over
Dishes whose finish is the point: browning, crisping, and reheating a single portion at a time.
What it changes about the day
One less thing competing for a rack and one temperature. That is the whole pitch, and it is enough.

Read a listing for the three things that matter on the day

Preset counts are noise; a preset is a saved time and temperature you can set by hand. Ignore them and look for three properties, each of which only matters if the listing states it for the model you are reading.

First, whether the cavity takes a flat tray at all. The 26-quart figure on the French-door machine in this edit is a cavity volume in the seller's own words — it is not a promise that your sheet pan fits, and it never will be. Second, whether the baskets or racks can run independently and finish together: the dual-basket listing here states two independent 3-quart baskets and a sync-finish function, in the seller's wording, which is the feature that matters when you are landing four dishes in one window. Third, whether you can see the food without opening it — the same listing words that as viewing windows.

If a property is not stated on the listing you have open, treat it as unknown. Do not import it from a review, a comment, or another model with a similar name.

A timing sheet you can fill in in five minutes

Sit-down time
Write the hour people eat. Everything else is derived from it.
Bird out and resting
The turkey guidance is to let the bird stand before carving. That window is free oven space — and the busiest twenty minutes for the second appliance.
The two hottest sides
Pick the two dishes that are ruined by going soft. Those are the ones that go in the countertop fryer last, not first.
The reheat run
Anything that was made ahead and needs to hit 165°F gets the final batch. Cover it, check it with a probe, and do not guess.
The queue check
Count your batches against your dishes. If the plan needs three sequential loads for four side dishes, you are not saving time. Move a dish back to the oven or change the menu.

Where this becomes a thermometer question

Every load-bearing number in this guide is measured with a probe, in the middle of food, at a moment when you are tired and the kitchen is full. That is the honest case for buying a thermometer alongside — or instead of — a second appliance. An instant-read answers “is this safe yet”; a leave-in probe answers “how close am I”, which is the one you want when the oven is blocked by a roasting pan.

The two products in this edit that address that are P-04, described by its listing as a digital meat thermometer, and P-05, described as a wireless meat thermometer whose stated uses include air frying. Neither is an air fryer, and this site has not cooked with either.
