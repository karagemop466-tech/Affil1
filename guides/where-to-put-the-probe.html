Where to put the thermometer probe, and why colour is not doneness

The most common thermometer error is not the thermometer. It is the placement, the assumption that a brown exterior means a safe interior, and a probe left somewhere it was never designed to stay.

Updated 2026-11-20 · Crisp & Counter

An editorial illustration of a handheld thermometer. It is not a photograph of a model sold on this site.

Placement is the part that gets the reading wrong

A thermometer is only right if it is in the right place. The published guidance is specific: put the probe in the thickest part of the food, away from bone, fat and gristle. For thin food — a burger patty, a thin chicken breast, a pork chop — insert it sideways through the side until the sensing area reaches the centre, which for a typical instant-read is roughly 2 to 3 inches.

For a whole bird, one spot is not enough: check the innermost part of the thigh, the innermost part of the wing, and the thickest part of the breast. For anything large or irregularly shaped, check in several places. And check each piece, because size and how food is distributed in the appliance means pieces reach a safe temperature at different times.

Why a brown exterior proves nothing

Colour and firmness are the signals people actually use, and they are the ones the guidance warns against. Some ground meat turns brown on the outside well before the inside is safe. In a USDA study of consumers grilling, 21% of participants relied on visual cues such as colour and grill marks to judge whether patties were done. The published position is that the only way to know food has reached a safe internal temperature is a food thermometer — a patty cooked to 160°F is safe regardless of colour.

There is a related trap for anyone reading appliance marketing: an air fryer's browning is a surface reaction. It tells you about the surface.

Instant-read or leave-in: two different questions

This is the decision most buyers get wrong, and the official thermometer guidance sorts it by design rather than by price. A leave-in probe is built to stay in the food while it cooks and reports temperature while you are doing something else. A handheld instant-read is used at the end, at the plate, and — importantly — most of them should not be left in food during cooking unless the manufacturer says it can withstand the heat and was designed for it.

That single line is worth more than any feature list. Accuracy is a range, not a number

Most food thermometers will read accurately within 2 to 4°F, according to the same guidance — which is why “it said 164” is not a reason to keep cooking a patty, and why “it said 172” is not a reason to panic. If you want to know whether yours is telling the truth, the two published checks are ice water, which should read 32°F with the stem at least 2 inches deep and not touching the glass, and boiling water, which should read 212°F at sea level after at least 30 seconds. Altitude changes the second number.

One more line from the same source, aimed squarely at connected-kitchen marketing: some models have plastic faces that can melt if placed too close to heat. Read the manufacturer's instructions before you leave anything in a closed, running appliance.
